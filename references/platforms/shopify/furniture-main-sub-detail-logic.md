# Furniture & Home Shopify Image Rules

When the task involves furniture and home products (sofas, chairs, tables, desks, shelving, lighting, rugs, bedding, decor, home office, etc.), default to using this logic. **Furniture imagery is spatial storytelling. The product is experienced through the room, not in isolation. Think Herman Miller, HAY, Muuto, Artek — not Wayfair marketplace thumbnails.**

## Applicable Categories

- **Seating**: Sofas, armchairs, dining chairs, office chairs, stools, benches, ottomans
- **Tables & Desks**: Dining tables, coffee tables, side tables, desks, consoles
- **Storage**: Shelving units, bookcases, cabinets, sideboards, credenzas, wardrobes
- **Lighting**: Floor lamps, table lamps, pendant lights, wall sconces, desk lamps
- **Bedding & Textiles**: Duvet covers, sheets, throws, cushions, blankets, curtains
- **Rugs**: Area rugs, runners, doormats
- **Decor**: Vases, mirrors, wall art, planters, candle holders, trays, objects
- **Home Office**: Desk + chair + storage systems, monitor stands, cable management, desk accessories
- **Outdoor**: Garden furniture, patio sets, outdoor lighting, planters

## The Core Philosophy

Furniture images fail for two reasons: (1) the product is isolated on white and feels like a CAD render, not something you'd live with, or (2) the styling is so busy the product disappears.

**The furniture Shopify image hierarchy is: Space → Proportion → Material → Detail.**

The viewer must understand the room before they understand the product. Scale is everything. A chair photographed in a vacuum tells you nothing about whether it's a lounge chair, a dining chair, or a child's chair. Place it in a room — now you know.

**The product is always the protagonist, but the room is its stage. Never photograph furniture without context.**

## Color Palette Rules

Furniture palettes are room-driven, not brand-driven:

- **Warm wood (oak, walnut, teak, beech)** → Warm neutrals: cream, sand, terracotta, olive, warm grey. Natural daylight from large windows. Linen, wool, ceramic textures.
- **Light wood (ash, birch, maple, pine)** → Scandinavian palette: white, pale grey, soft pastels. Diffused northern light. Paper, cotton, light wool textures.
- **Dark / black furniture** → Moody interiors: charcoal, deep green, navy, warm black. Window light with deep shadows. Velvet, brass, dark wood accents. But never so dark the product form is lost.
- **Colored / upholstered pieces** → The upholstery IS the color anchor. Build the room palette around it — complementary neutrals, one accent color maximum. Never compete with the upholstery.
- **Metal / glass / industrial** → Cool tones: concrete grey, steel blue, white. Hard daylight or diffused skylight. Concrete, glass, steel textures.

**Universal rule**: The room's color palette has 3-4 colors maximum including the product. A styled furniture shot is a color composition, not a color collection.

## Default Recommended Set Style

```markdown
For furniture, space is the product. I recommend Option 1 — architectural context:
1. Architectural Interior (Recommended): Product placed in a considered, light-filled interior with architectural detail — tall windows, wooden floors, plaster walls, high ceilings. Wide establishing shots communicate scale; detail shots reveal material, proportion, and joinery. Palette follows the room: warm neutrals, natural light, layered textures. Think Kinfolk interiors, not IKEA catalog.
2. Studio Isolation: Product on a seamless backdrop (warm grey, sand, or clay tone — never pure white) with precise soft shadow work. Best for variant/color swatch images or catalog-style product comparison. But never use this as the ONLY image type — always pair with room context.
3. Material Vignette: Extreme close-ups of wood grain, fabric weave, metal finish, leather patina, joinery detail. These are texture portraits. Paired with room shots for scale. Think of it as "material storytelling" — the viewer sees the craftsmanship before they see the price.
4. Lifestyle Narrative: Fully styled room scenes with a curated layer of objects — books, ceramics, plants, art, textiles. The product is the protagonist, not just furniture. Best for DTC brands selling an entire aesthetic, not individual pieces.

If the merchant doesn't specify, I'll default to Option 1.
```

## Default Main Image Logic

Main image recommended 1:1, for search results, collection pages, product card primary.

### Visual Structure

- **Wide establishing shot or tight architectural crop.** The product occupies 40%-60% of frame — lower than other categories. The room context IS the product value.
- Furniture main images are the only Shopify category where the product should NOT dominate the frame. A sofa cropped tight to fill 80% of a 1:1 square looks like a warehouse listing. Pull back. Show the floor. Show the wall. Show the light coming in.
- Background: must be a real interior (or simulated-real). No gradients. No flat color backgrounds. Wood floors, plaster walls, window light, rug, a single piece of art, a single plant.
- Lighting: natural daylight, diffused, warm. Late morning or golden hour. No harsh shadows. No studio flash that flattens texture. **Furniture needs shadow to read as three-dimensional.**
- Text on main image: optional. If used, 1 line maximum — product name in small, elegant serif or refined sans-serif, placed low and subtle. Never center-aligned sales copy.

### Main Image Prompt Framework

```text
Generate a Shopify furniture main image, 1:1 ratio.

Product: {product type, name, dimensions, material, color, upholstery, finish, key design details}. Product reference locks exact design — proportions, leg style, arm shape, back height, cushion configuration, material color/texture.

Scene: An architectural interior with {describe room type, floor material, wall color, window placement, quality of light}. The space must feel aspirational but lived-in — curated, not staged. The room establishes scale and context for the product.

Lighting: Natural daylight through {windows / skylight / glass doors}. Warm, diffused, directional but soft. Late morning feel. Subtle shadows that give the furniture three-dimensional form. No studio flash.

Composition: The product occupies 40%-60% of frame. The room is visible — floor, walls, window, at least one contextual element ({rug / plant / art piece / side table}). The product is the protagonist but the room is its world. Depth of field should keep the product sharp while allowing background to softly recede.

On-image copy (optional): Product name only — small, {bottom-left / bottom-right}, in a refined serif or elegant sans-serif. No more than one line.

Style references: Herman Miller catalog, HAY lookbooks, Muuto product pages, Kinfolk interiors, Architectural Digest home features. Editorial spatial photography, not e-commerce product photography.

Requirements: No text beyond optional product name. No price stickers, no sale badges, no "Free Shipping" icons. The furniture's proportions, material, and color must be accurate. No watermark. Room must feel like a real architectural space, not a studio set.
```

## Default Gallery Image Logic

Gallery recommended 5-7 images, mixed ratios (1:1 + 3:4 + 16:9 for wide room shots). Each image reveals a new layer of the product experience.

### Gallery 1: Alternate Room Angle

**Goal**: Show the product from a different viewpoint within the same room.

- Different angle (90° or opposite corner). Same room, same light, same styling.
- Reveals a different face of the furniture — the side profile, the back, or how it relates to a different part of the room.
- This proves the product works from every angle, not just the hero shot.
- **No text.**

### Gallery 2: Detail — Material & Craft

**Goal**: Make the viewer appreciate what they're paying for.

- Extreme close-up: wood grain with finish, fabric weave at thread level, leather grain and stitching, metal joint or weld, marble veining, ceramic glaze.
- Shallow depth of field. Soft natural light. The image should make the viewer want to reach through the screen.
- 1 short label acceptable: "Solid American Walnut" / "Hand-stitched Italian Leather" / "Stonewashed Belgian Linen."

### Gallery 3: Detail — Joinery / Construction

**Goal**: Show how it's made. This is for design-literate buyers.

- Close-up of a joint, leg attachment, arm-to-frame connection, drawer dovetail, cushion seam, zipper detail, hardware (handle, hinge, foot, glide).
- Tight crop. The focus is precision engineering or artisanal hand-work.
- Optional small label: "Mortise and tenon joinery" / "Solid brass hardware."

### Gallery 4: Styled Vignette

**Goal**: Show the product styled as part of a life.

- Tighter shot than the main image — focus on the product + objects immediately around it.
- A chair with a throw blanket and a book on the seat. A coffee table with a ceramic vase, a candle, and a design book. A bed with layered pillows and a side table with a lamp.
- The styling must feel curated, not cluttered. 3-5 objects maximum, all in the same material language as the product.
- This is the "how it lives" image.

### Gallery 5: In Use / Human Scale

**Goal**: Provide scale reference and emotional connection.

- A person interacting with the furniture — sitting in the chair, working at the desk, reaching for a book on the shelf, lying on the sofa.
- The person is secondary to the product. They exist to show scale and use, not to be a fashion subject.
- Partial body is preferred: hands on an armrest, legs stretched on an ottoman, back view at a desk. Full body only if necessary for scale.
- **No direct eye contact with the camera.** The person is part of the scene, not the subject.

### Gallery 6: Dimensions / Spec Visualization

**Goal**: Communicate size and configuration — elegantly.

- Product shown with dimension lines (clean, thin, architectural-drawing style).
- Or: product in a room with a human silhouette for scale reference.
- Or: configuration options shown (modular sofa arrangements, table extension leaves, shelving configurations).
- Text: dimension numbers in architectural font (thin, precise). Not a busy spec table.
- This is a design drawing brought into photography, not an infographic.

### Gallery 7: Variants / Materials

**Goal**: Show all finishes and materials — as a design system, not a product grid.

- All variants in the same room setting, same angle, same light. Or presented as material swatches with small in-situ images.
- Color/material names beneath each. "Oak — Natural Oil Finish" / "Walnut — Dark Stain."
- If too many variants for one frame, split by material family (woods in one, fabrics in another).

## Default PDP Logic

Furniture PDP is an architectural walkthrough, not a feature list. Each screen is a room in the viewer's imagination.

### 7-Screen PDP Structure

1. **Hero Space**: Wide architectural shot. Product in its ideal room. The headline is one line about design intention, not features: "A chair that disappears into the room." Not "Ergonomic lumbar support with adjustable armrests." Massive negative space around the copy.
2. **Design Story**: The "why." Material philosophy, designer background (if notable), design intention. Product shot from its most sculptural angle. Copy is narrative, not bullet points. 2-3 sentences maximum.
3. **Material Chapter**: Two or three macro texture shots in a row (or one composed multi-texture image). Wood, fabric, metal, leather. Each with a short material name. This screen is about sensory promise.
4. **Craft Chapter**: Joinery, construction, details. Close-ups of how it's made. The message: this isn't mass-produced; someone made this well.
5. **In the Room**: Lifestyle context — the product in a fully realized room. This is the emotional peak of the PDP. Let the image do the work; one short line of copy.
6. **Dimensions & Fit**: Clean dimension diagram. Product with human scale reference. Room size suggestions. "Fits a 200 sq ft living room" type guidance. Information-forward but visually clean.
7. **The Collection / Close**: Product hero shot returns. If the piece is part of a collection, show family. Configuration/variant summary. Purchase trigger: "Configure yours."

### PDP Coherence Rules (Furniture Edition)

- **Same room across all screens.** If the hero is in a light-filled loft with oak floors and white walls, that room persists. You're walking around the same space, discovering the product from different vantage points.
- **Same light quality.** If the hero has warm morning light from east-facing windows, detail shots have the same warmth even at macro scale. No cold studio light suddenly appearing.
- **Same styling language across the set.** If the hero room has a linen throw, ceramic vase, and olive tree, those objects (or their material siblings) recur. The viewer subconsciously registers this as "one world."
- **Typography is spatial, not graphic.** Furniture PDP typography should be architectural — thin, well-spaced, placed with the precision of a blueprint. Serifs (think editorial magazines) or refined geometric sans-serifs (think gallery wall labels). Never display fonts.

```text
Generate a Shopify furniture PDP screen #{N}, 3:4 or 16:9 ratio.

This screen: {hero space / design story / material chapter / craft chapter / in the room / dimensions / collection close}.

Product: {exact description — type, dimensions, materials, color, finish, configuration}. Product reference locks exact design. Every product appearance across all PDP screens must be pixel-consistent.

Visual system: Room {loft / apartment / house — describe architecture, floors, walls, windows}. Lighting {time of day, direction, quality — warm morning / diffused afternoon / golden hour}. Styling objects {describe the curated layer — consistent across screens}. Typography {editorial serif or refined geometric sans — one family throughout}.

Composition: {Screen-specific layout — wide architectural shot / macro texture / lifestyle frame / clean dimension diagram / product hero}.

Copy: Headline "{short line — design philosophy, not feature}". Sub-line "{optional one sentence}". Labels "{material names, dimension numbers — only if needed}".

Requirements: Must feel like walking through the same space, discovering the product from different distances. No price tags, no sale badges, no "Free Shipping." Museum or gallery-level curation. No watermark.
```

## Variant Image Logic

Furniture variants are material stories:

- 2-3 images per material/color variant: **wide room shot** + **detail texture** + (optionally) **alternate angle**.
- Room shot must be identical in composition, lighting, and styling across variants — only the product material/color changes. This lets the buyer compare without cognitive load.
- Texture detail shot is unique to each variant — show the specific wood grain, fabric weave, or leather finish.
- Color/material name beneath each image: "Walnut — Natural Oil" / "Oak — White Soap Finish" / "Mohair — Terracotta."
- If the product is upholstered, fabric swatch close-ups are essential. People buy upholstery with their eyes, then their hands — the image must do the hand's work.

## Promotional Banner Logic

Furniture promo banners must never feel like a sale. The furniture brand that screams "SALE" has already lost the premium buyer.

- Campaign theme should be seasonal or occasion-driven: "The Summer Collection," "Designed for Living," "New Works." Never "Biggest Sale of the Year" as a primary message.
- If a price promotion exists, bury it — small type, tasteful placement, not the visual anchor.
- Colors stay within the room palette. A red "SALE" badge over a walnut and linen living room is visual violence.
- Imagery uses the same room context, same lighting, same styling as the main/gallery set. The banner is an extension of the brand, not an interruption.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

1. **White background isolation.** A chair on pure white looks like a returns-eligible Amazon listing, not a $2,400 design piece. Always show the floor.
2. **No scale reference.** Furniture without context is furniture without meaning. Always include something that communicates size — a window, a doorframe, a book, a hand.
3. **Over-styling.** A coffee table with 17 objects on it is a styling exercise, not a product image. The product must be findable within 1 second.
4. **Under-styling.** A sofa in an empty white room is a showroom, not a home. 3-5 curated objects turn "product" into "aspiration."
5. **Inconsistent light across the set.** If the wide shot has warm morning light and the detail shot looks like a dental office, the set is broken.
6. **CAD / render-looking images.** Furniture images that look too perfect (no shadows, no texture variation, no lived-in quality) feel fake. Premium brands embrace imperfection — wood grain variation, fabric slubs, soft wrinkles in linen.

## One-Line Summary

Furniture imagery is not product photography — it's spatial storytelling: **the room sells the piece, the detail justifies the price, the light makes it real. Never show furniture without showing where it lives.**
